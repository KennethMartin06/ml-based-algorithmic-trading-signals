## 2. Can a model learn when to be in the market?

*Transition: morph* · *clicks to advance builds: 0*

Here is the core idea. On the left is a rule a human writes once: buy when the 10-day average is above the 50-day average. On the right, instead of a fixed rule, a model looks at 42 features and estimates the probability that the next move is up, and we invest only when that probability clears a threshold tau. The important design decision is fairness: both approaches get exactly the same data, the same execution timing and the same trading costs, so any difference really comes from the signal. We test this on three liquid instruments: SPY, an exchange-traded fund that tracks the S&P 500, plus Apple and Microsoft, using daily prices from 2010 to 2025.

## 3. Four easy ways to fool yourself in a backtest

*Transition: fade* · *clicks to advance builds: 0*

Why is this hard? Because a trading backtest is very easy to get wrong in ways that make results look better than they are. First, look-ahead bias, where some feature or label secretly uses future data. Second, random cross-validation on time series, which lets the model learn from the future of the days it is tested on. Third, ignoring trading costs and execution timing. And fourth, weak baselines: a model at 51 percent sounds better than a coin flip, but simply predicting 'up' every day already scores 49.5% on SPY. Our framework was designed to close all four of these traps.

## 4. Small evaluation choices flip the conclusion

*Transition: fade* · *clicks to advance builds: 0*

Here is why this matters, using our own results. The chart shows a naive 'tomorrow repeats today' strategy. A quick definition first: the Sharpe ratio is the annualised return divided by the annualised volatility, so it measures return per unit of risk, and a basis point is a hundredth of a percent. With zero costs this strategy actually has a positive Sharpe ratio on all three tickers; at realistic costs it becomes deeply negative. On the right: the same Random Forest on Apple goes from a Sharpe of 0.58 to 0.01 just by changing the assumed fill price from the next open to the same day's close. And the 95 percent interval on buy-and-hold's own Sharpe ratio runs from −0.21 to 1.58. Four years of data is a small sample, so we need statistics, not single numbers.

## 6. A leakage-safe, cost-aware comparison framework

*Transition: fade* · *clicks to advance builds: 0*

Our solution maps one safeguard to each trap. Against look-ahead bias, every feature is a point-in-time, scale-invariant ratio, and our tests recompute features on truncated data and require identical values. Against leaky validation, splits are strictly chronological with a two-day purge, and tuning uses purged time-series cross-validation for the tabular models and a purged hold-out slice for the LSTM. Against free trading, we decide at the close, fill at the next open, and charge 15 basis points on every unit of turnover. Against weak baselines, we compare with four baselines and report bootstrap confidence intervals.

## 9. Time only flows forward: chronological splits

*Transition: fade* · *clicks to advance builds: 0*

Time only flows forward, so our splits do too. We train on 2011 to 2019, which is 2,243 trading days, validate on 2020 and 2021, and keep 1,001 test days from 2022 to 2025 that are never used for fitting or for choosing anything. Hyperparameters are tuned inside the training period only: purged time-series cross-validation for the tabular models, and a purged hold-out slice for the LSTM. The validation period chooses the trading threshold and the ensemble members. Because each label looks two opens ahead, we purge the last two rows before each boundary so no label overlaps the next segment.

## 10. Walk-forward: retrain every year, trade the next

*Transition: morph* · *clicks to advance builds: 0*

A single split could just be lucky, so we also ran a walk-forward test. The bars you just saw move to the bottom row and become one of 10 folds. For every test year from 2016 to 2025, we retrain on all data up to two years before it, choose the threshold and the ensemble on the year just before it, and then trade the test year out-of-sample. The tabular models are re-tuned in every fold; the LSTM keeps its architecture and re-tunes only its class weighting. The training window grows each year, like a real trader who keeps learning. This gives ten years of out-of-sample trading for each ticker.

## 11. 42 point-in-time features, one executable target

*Transition: fade* · *clicks to advance builds: 0*

On the left are the 42 features in five groups: returns, technical indicators such as RSI, MACD and Bollinger bands, volatility, volume and market regime. All of them are ratios rather than price levels, so adjusted prices cannot leak future information, and a test checks this. On the right is the target. After the close on day t we compute features and predict; we can only trade at the next open, t plus one, and the position is held until the following open. So the return we predict is open t+2 over open t+1, split into Down, Neutral and Up with a plus or minus 0.1 percent band.

## 12. Five model families, tuned without peeking

*Transition: fade* · *clicks to advance builds: 0*

We trained five model families: Logistic Regression, Random Forest, XGBoost, a small LSTM in PyTorch, and a soft-vote ensemble whose two members are chosen on the validation set. All tuning used log loss inside the training period: purged time-series cross-validation for the tabular models, and a purged hold-out slice for the LSTM. The selected values are telling: Logistic Regression chose C = 0.001, the strongest regularisation in its grid, the trees chose depth 2 or 3, and the LSTM chose a short window. When tuning keeps choosing the simplest model, it usually means there is little stable signal to learn, which already hints at the results. We also built four baselines.

## 13. From probability to position, net of costs

*Transition: fade* · *clicks to advance builds: 0*

Here is how a probability becomes a trade. If the model's probability of an up move is at least tau, we go long at the next open; otherwise we hold cash. Tau is picked from 14 values using validation Sharpe only; the test set never influences it. The backtester on the right charges costs on every change in position, 15 basis points per unit of turnover, before applying the day's return, and it compounds equity day by day. Every strategy, baselines included, goes through exactly this code, and we report the full set of metrics for each.

## 19. Walk-forward and robustness checks agree

*Transition: fade* · *clicks to advance builds: 0*

The walk-forward test tells the same story over ten years. Buy-and-hold is the white bar, the moving-average crossover is grey, and amber is the best ML model for each ticker, picked with hindsight. Even with that cherry-picking, ML never beat buy-and-hold: 0 of 15. The robustness checks on the right use the 2022 to 2025 hold-out test, and they agree. With zero costs, 7 strategies edge ahead, but at 40 basis points none do. Volatility targeting cut drawdowns by 21 to 44 percent at a similar Sharpe ratio, simply by holding less when markets are volatile; that is risk control, not prediction. And changing the target or the fill price changes which strategies 'win', a classic sign of noise.

## 20. Challenges we handled, limitations we report

*Transition: fade* · *clicks to advance builds: 0*

On the left, the main engineering challenges we handled. Leakage can creep in at every step, so we built tests that fail on any look-ahead. Thresholds chosen on a bull-market validation period often collapse to buy-and-hold, and we report that honestly. The LSTM is unstable with only about 2,200 training days, so we kept it small. Live data can include today's unfinished bar, which we now drop. On the right are the limitations we state openly: a weak daily signal, survivorship bias from choosing successful stocks, many comparisons without a multiple-testing correction, a simple cost model, and price data only.

## 22. What we can claim

*Transition: fade* · *clicks to advance builds: 0*

To conclude, three claims we can defend. First, at a daily horizon, machine learning did not reliably beat simple baselines, either on accuracy or on risk-adjusted returns, which is consistent with how efficient liquid large caps are.
